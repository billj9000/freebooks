Changing a Form's Layout

A form — an invoice, a purchase order, a statement, a cheque — is a set of items placed at particular positions on the page, rather than a list of columns like a report. The Form Layout view shows you where each of those items sits, drawn on top of the real printed page.

Open it from Tools → Form Layout, or with the Layout button beside a form on the same page. It needs the same permission as the report editor: Forms — write a report.

Opened with nothing chosen, this page is the list of every form the company has, grouped by folder — Invoices/Packing Slips, Purchase Orders, Customer Statements, and so on. Reports are listed separately, under Tools → Reports (Forms), because running a report and laying out a form have no action in common.

What you are looking at

The page in the middle is the form as it will actually print — the same page the Print button produces, not a preview drawn to look like it. Over it sits a coloured outline around every element, with a different colour for each kind: fixed text, a value from the record, an address block, the table of line items, lines, boxes, images, page numbers and barcodes.

Point at an outline and it thickens, and the matching entry in the Elements list on the right highlights. Point at a row in that list and the outline on the page highlights instead, which is the quicker way round when you are hunting for one particular field on a crowded form. Resting on an outline also shows its position and size in millimetres.

Untick Show the elements to take the outlines away and see the page on its own. That is the way to check that a box really is over its text rather than merely near it.

Boxes with a dashed lower edge

An address block encloses only its first line, and that is correct rather than a fault. What is stored for a block of text is where it starts and how tall one line is; the rest of the address flows on below, and how far it reaches depends on the address — a customer with a company name and a county needs more lines than one without.

Those boxes are drawn with a dashed lower edge to show that the text continues past them. It applies to any text that can run to more than one line: address and company blocks always, and an ordinary field whenever its value is too long for the width given.

This matters when you come to move things. The space an address block needs on the page is not the box you can see, so leaving room underneath it is part of positioning it.

A box drawn with a dashed edge all the way round means something different: the size could not be worked out at all. That happens when a form takes its logo from the record rather than naming a fixed file, or when the image file is missing. The position is right; the size is only indicative.

Zoom and pages

The + and − buttons zoom, and the arrows move between pages on a form long enough to need more than one. The outlines follow the zoom exactly; if they ever drift away from the text underneath at some zoom levels, that is worth reporting, because they are calculated from the same measurements the printer uses.

When the figures look invented

If your company has no record this form would print — a new installation with no invoices yet, for instance — the page is filled with made-up values so that there is something to look at, and a note at the top says so. The layout is the real one; only the data is not. Your company name and address are still real, because those are settings rather than data.

Moving things

Drag an element to move it. To change its size, take hold of one of its edges or corners: a corner changes the width and the height together, and an edge changes only the side you are pulling, leaving the opposite one where it is. The pointer tells you which you have — a double-headed arrow, straight or diagonal, wherever a drag will resize rather than move. Each corner carries a small mark.

Pulling the top or left edge moves the element as well as resizing it, which is what you want when it is the bottom or the right that has to stay put.

A very thin element — a horizontal rule is only a line thick — offers no grip on the side it has no thickness on, so that it can still be picked up and moved. Drag its ends to make it longer or shorter, and type in the panel on the right for anything else.

The page redraws itself each time you finish a change, so what you see is the document as it would really print. Nothing is saved by redrawing.

It redraws when you let go of an element, not while you are dragging it — there is no point producing a page you are covering with the pointer. Typing in the panel on the right redraws a moment after you stop.

If it feels slow, untick Auto redraw and use the Redraw the page button instead. That choice is remembered on this computer. With redrawing off, remember that after the first change the picture no longer matches what you have, and every change after that is being judged against a page further out of date.

Snap to rounds every move to the nearest half, one or five millimetres, which is how you get a column of fields lined up exactly. Set it to Off for fine adjustments.

Everything can also be typed. Select an element and the panel on the right shows its exact position and size in millimetres, its font, size, colour and alignment, its border, and which page it appears on. Typing there is the way to make two elements agree precisely.

Elements that sit on top of one another

Forms are crowded, and much of what is on one sits inside something else: a frame drawn around a block of fields, a shaded panel behind a heading. Clicking takes the smallest element under the pointer, so clicking a field inside a frame selects the field, which is almost always what was meant.

To select the frame itself, click a part of it that no smaller element covers — the space between the fields will do. Once it is selected, its corners and sides go on working for resizing even where a field stands in front of them.

An element completely covered by another is reached from the Elements list. Run the pointer down the list and the matching element lights up on the page, so you can tell which row is which before you click it.

Moving several at once

Most tidying moves a group rather than a single item: a caption and the value beneath it, an address and its heading, the several parts of a cheque stub. Moved one at a time they drift out of line with each other, so it is worth selecting them together.

There are two ways to do it. Hold Shift and click each element in turn — on the page or in the Elements list — and each click adds one to the selection, or takes it out again if it was already in. Or drag across an empty part of the page: a dashed box follows the pointer, and every element that falls entirely inside it is selected. An element the box merely crosses is not selected, so to catch a full-width rule the box has to reach past both of its ends.

Then drag any one of them and they all move together, keeping their positions relative to one another exactly. Delete and the forward and backward arrows apply to the whole selection too.

A selected element is drawn with a dark ring around it. The one the panel on the right is describing — the last one you clicked — has a solid ring; the others are dashed. Typing in the panel changes only the one with the solid ring, and when more than one is selected the panel says so.

Click any element without holding Shift to go back to having just that one selected, or click an empty part of the page to select nothing.

Resizing always applies to the single element whose edge you took hold of, even when several are selected.

Naming an element

The Name box at the top of the panel is what the element is called in the Elements list. It is optional: leave it empty and the element is described by what it is — a piece of text by its words, a value by the field it shows, the line-item table by its source.

That description is enough for most things and no help at all for a picture, because every image is simply called image. On a form with a logo and a signature, naming them is what lets you pick the right one out of the list. Names are worth giving to anything you will come back to: an address block called Ship to is found instantly on a form carrying seventy elements.

Clearing the name puts the description back. The name is only a label — it is never printed on the document.

On a text element the height box is labelled Line height, because that is what it is: the height of one line, not of the whole element. See the note about dashed edges above.

Borders, and how thick a line or a box is drawn

Border width is in points, and 0 does not mean "no border" — it means a hairline, the thinnest line the printer can manage. Anything below about 0.25 comes out looking the same as 0 on paper. 1 is the usual thin rule, and is what is used when no width is set.

Draw it is what turns a border on and off, and it only appears for the elements that have one to turn off: text, values, address blocks, page numbers, the line-item table and barcodes.

A box and a line are an outline — they always draw one — so on those two the panel says Thickness in place of Border width. It is the same setting and the same points, named for what it does there: it is how thick the rule or the frame is drawn, and it is the only size a line or a box has. The font, size and alignment boxes are not shown for them, because nothing on a line or a box is drawn in a font and there is no text to line up. A new line or box starts at 1 point, which you can then change to anything you like.

The border has its own colour, separate from the text. Leave it and the border follows the text colour; set it and the two are independent, which is how the shipped invoice draws black text inside a silver rule.

A line and a box show one colour rather than two, for the same reason they show Thickness rather than Border width: there is no text on them for a second colour to belong to. The swatch is whatever the element is actually drawn in, and changing it changes what you see. On older forms the two settings could disagree, and the border colour was always the one that won — so if a line or a box looks a colour you did not choose, that swatch is now the one to change.

Text of more than one line

The Text box holds as many lines as you like — press Enter for a new one. A block of bank details, a payment-methods note or a registered-address footer is one text element, not one per line.

Give the element enough width for the longest line. Height is the height of one line rather than of the whole block, so the text carries on below the outline you see on the page; the dashed bottom edge is the page saying so.

Printing something only on some records

By default every element prints on every record the form is run for. Prints when changes that: choose a value, a test and something to compare against, and the element is printed only for records that pass the test. Leave it as Always — which is how every element starts — and nothing changes.

This is how one invoice carries different bank details for different currencies. Put three text elements in the same place on the page, one above the other, and give each a different test:

  • the sterling account, where Currency is one of GBP
  • the euro account, where Currency is one of EUR
  • the account for everything else, where Currency is none of GBP,EUR

Every invoice then prints exactly one of the three. is none of is how you cover "anything I have not listed" — without it a customer paying in a currency nobody thought of would get no bank details at all.

The tests are:

  • is and is not — an exact match. Capital letters do not matter, so eur and EUR are the same thing.
  • is one of and is none of — several values, separated by commas.
  • is blank and is not blank — whether the record has any value there at all. These take nothing to compare against, so the box disappears.
  • is more than and is less than — numbers compare as numbers, and dates as dates. Balance due is more than 0 is how you print an overdue notice only on invoices that are still owed; is 0 on a second element is how you print PAID on the rest.

Only the values this form can supply are offered, the same list the Shows box uses. A condition works on any kind of element, not only text — a box, a line, a barcode, a logo and the line-item table can each be given one.

Seeing which one will print

Elements with a condition are marked in the element list with a small diamond, which is how you tell three stacked elements apart when only one outline is visible on the page.

On the page itself, an element that would not print for the record shown underneath is faded. The picture behind the outlines is one real record from your company, so with a sterling invoice showing you will see the sterling block drawn solid and the other two faded. They are still there, still selectable, and still saved — fading says only "not this one".

Showing, above the page, is which record you are looking at. Pick another from the list to see the form drawn for it — that is how you check a faded element by going and finding a record it does print for.

The list holds the most recent records of the kind this form prints. Laying out a sales invoice, you are offered sales invoices — not the sales orders, receipts and purchase orders that share the same underlying table.

When the one you want is not among them — the euro invoice you raised in March — type its number in the box beside the list and press Show. Any reference works: an invoice number, an order number, whatever your company numbers that kind of document by. The search is restricted the same way, so a number that belongs to a purchase order will not be offered while you are editing an invoice.

Two things the page will tell you rather than leave you guessing. If nothing matches what you typed, it says so. And if the record you chose is one this form cannot print — a purchase order picked while you are laying out an invoice — it draws a different one and says that too, so a page that did not change is never a mystery.

Changing the record does not touch your layout. Unsaved changes stay exactly as they were, and the page is redrawn with them.

Adding, removing and overlapping

Add an element offers all nine kinds: fixed text, a value, an address block, the table of line items, a line, a box, an image, a page number and a barcode. New elements appear near the top-left corner — drag them where you want them.

A value and a barcode must each be given something to show, chosen from the list of values this form can supply; nothing else can be typed in, and one added without a value chosen is not kept when you save. An address block and a line-item table are the opposite: they are added empty, because what goes in them is a list rather than a single choice, and you fill that list in the panel described below. Until you do, they draw nothing.

This is what makes a form built from scratch worth building. A form needs its line items and its customer address before it is an invoice at all, and until these could be added, starting from a blank form meant starting from something that could never become one.

A new image starts out empty and draws nothing until you give it a picture, which is the next section. Its height is left unset on purpose, so that the shape comes from whatever picture you choose rather than from the size it happened to be added at.

The arrows beside a selected element bring it forward or send it backward. That matters where two elements overlap: a filled box drawn after a piece of text hides it. With several elements selected they all move a step together, keeping their order among themselves; if any one of them is already at the end, none of them moves.

Delete removes everything selected, and says how many before it does. The Delete key on the keyboard does the same, as does Backspace — which is the key marked "delete" on a Mac keyboard. Either way you are asked first.

The key does nothing while you are typing in a box, or while the column or line list is open, so it never removes an element out from under whatever you were actually working on.

The columns of the line-item table

Select the line-item table and the panel offers Columns, with the number it has. That opens the list of them at full width, because a column has five things to say about it and the panel on the right is too narrow to say them clearly.

Each column shows one value from a line of the document — the item code, its description, the quantity, the price. Alongside it you set the heading printed above the column, its width in millimetres, whether the text sits left, centre or right, and a format.

The format is worth a moment, because an empty one prints the value exactly as it is stored: a price held as 12.5 prints "12.5". For a line-item table you will usually want posted_cur on a price or a total, null_pcur where a zero should print as nothing, rnd_dec on a quantity and date on a date. Text columns need nothing. The formats are a list you choose from, grouped; the full explanation of each is in Value Formats.

The order of the rows is the order the columns print in, left to right, and the arrows beside each row are how you change it. The cross removes a column.

A table with no columns prints nothing, and the list says so while it is empty. That is the state a newly added table starts in, so it is worth adding the first column straight away.

The lines of an address block

An address block is several values printed as one block of text: the customer's name, then the first line of the address, then the town, and so on. Select one and the panel offers Lines, which opens the same kind of list.

Each row is one value, and beside it is Followed by — what joins it to the row beneath. This is the setting that makes an address look like an address:

  • A new line puts the next value on its own line.
  • A new line, unless it is empty does the same, but closes the gap up when the value is blank. This is what you want for a second address line that many customers do not have; with a plain new line, those customers get a blank line in the middle of their address.
  • A comma and a space, a space, and the rest join the two on the same line, which is how a town and a postcode end up together.
  • Nothing runs the two straight together with no gap at all.

The last row's setting has nothing after it to join to, so it makes no difference.

Each line has a format as well, which does the same job it does in the table: an amount or a date in an address block needs one, and a name or a street does not. See Value Formats.

Some lines of a company block show one of your own company details rather than something from the record — your name, your address, your telephone number. Those rows say so in words instead of offering a list, and they cannot be pointed at anything else here; change them in Company Settings. You can still reorder them and change how they are joined, which is the usual reason for opening the list at all.

Page numbers and barcodes

A page number's Wording is what it prints, with three substitutions: {page} for the number of the page, {pages} for how many there are, and {of} for the word "of" in your language. So {page} {of} {pages} prints "2 of 5", and {page} / {pages} prints "2 / 5".

A barcode has a Barcode type. Every form converted from the old designer uses Code 128, which is the general-purpose choice and encodes anything; change it only if the scanner that will read it needs a particular one. A type the printer cannot draw is refused when you save rather than left to fail at the printer.

Logos and other images

Select an image element — either one the form already has, or one you have just added — and the panel offers the images this company already has, and an Add an image box for putting a new one there. Choose the file, click Upload, and the selected element uses it straight away — the page redraws so you can see how it sits.

So a form that was never given a logo can be given one: add an image, drag it into the corner, and upload the file.

PNG, JPEG and GIF are accepted, up to 4 MB. Anything else is refused, and so is a file that merely ends in .png without being one. The image belongs to this company alone; other companies on the same installation do not see it.

The name it is stored under may not be quite the name you chose. Unusual characters are removed, the ending is set from what the file actually is, and if that name is already taken a number is added rather than the existing image being replaced — another form may be using it.

Most images are stored with a width and no height, so that the shape always comes from the file. Uploading a differently proportioned image therefore changes how tall the element is, and dragging its left or right edge changes the height along with the width, keeping the picture in proportion. Set both a width and a height if you need the space it occupies to stay fixed — dragging the top or bottom edge does exactly that, since it fixes a height that was not set before.

Some forms take their image from the record rather than from a fixed file — a signature or a logo that differs per customer. Those offer a value to show instead of a file, and there is nothing to upload.

Undo

Undo steps back through your changes one at a time, and Redo puts one back. Ctrl+Z and Ctrl+Y do the same. A drag counts as one change however far you moved the element — and however many elements you moved — so undoing returns them to where they started rather than partway.

Selecting things is not a change and is not recorded, but undoing does put back the selection you had at the time, so you can carry on where you were.

Inside a text box, Ctrl+Z is the box's own undo for the text you are typing, as usual.

Undo covers the changes you have made since opening the form, and that history is lost when you leave the page. It does not undo a save: to go back further than this, leave without saving and open the form again.

Saving

Save rewrites the form in the Forms format. If it was still in the older format you will see a note before you save: afterwards the original print engine can no longer open that form, and a copy of the original is kept alongside it.

The editor only saves what it can show you. The few things it has no controls for — where a line-item table draws its rows from, the styling of that table's heading row — are kept exactly as they were.
