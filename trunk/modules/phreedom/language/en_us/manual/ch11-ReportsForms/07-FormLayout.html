Changing a Form's Layout

A form — an invoice, a purchase order, a statement, a cheque — is a set of items placed at particular positions on the page, rather than a list of columns like a report. The Form Layout view shows you where each of those items sits, drawn on top of the real printed page.

Open it from Tools → Form Layout, or with the Layout button beside a form on Tools → Documents (Forms). It needs the same permission as the report editor: Forms — write a report.

What you are looking at

The page in the middle is the form as it will actually print — the same page the Print button produces, not a preview drawn to look like it. Over it sits a coloured outline around every element, with a different colour for each kind: fixed text, a value from the record, an address block, the table of line items, lines, boxes, images, page numbers and barcodes.

Point at an outline and it thickens, and the matching entry in the Elements list on the right highlights. Point at a row in that list and the outline on the page highlights instead, which is the quicker way round when you are hunting for one particular field on a crowded form. Resting on an outline also shows its position and size in millimetres.

Untick Show the elements to take the outlines away and see the page on its own. That is the way to check that a box really is over its text rather than merely near it.

Boxes with a dashed lower edge

An address block encloses only its first line, and that is correct rather than a fault. What is stored for a block of text is where it starts and how tall one line is; the rest of the address flows on below, and how far it reaches depends on the address — a customer with a company name and a county needs more lines than one without.

Those boxes are drawn with a dashed lower edge to show that the text continues past them. It applies to any text that can run to more than one line: address and company blocks always, and an ordinary field whenever its value is too long for the width given.

This matters when you come to move things. The space an address block needs on the page is not the box you can see, so leaving room underneath it is part of positioning it.

A box drawn with a dashed edge all the way round means something different: the size could not be worked out at all. That happens when a form takes its logo from the record rather than naming a fixed file, or when the image file is missing. The position is right; the size is only indicative.

Zoom and pages

The + and − buttons zoom, and the arrows move between pages on a form long enough to need more than one. The outlines follow the zoom exactly; if they ever drift away from the text underneath at some zoom levels, that is worth reporting, because they are calculated from the same measurements the printer uses.

When the figures look invented

If your company has no record this form would print — a new installation with no invoices yet, for instance — the page is filled with made-up values so that there is something to look at, and a note at the top says so. The layout is the real one; only the data is not. Your company name and address are still real, because those are settings rather than data.

Moving things

Drag an element to move it, or drag its bottom-right corner to make it wider or taller. The page redraws itself each time you finish a change, so what you see is the document as it would really print. Nothing is saved by redrawing.

It redraws when you let go of an element, not while you are dragging it — there is no point producing a page you are covering with the pointer. Typing in the panel on the right redraws a moment after you stop.

If it feels slow, untick Auto redraw and use the Redraw the page button instead. That choice is remembered on this computer. With redrawing off, remember that after the first change the picture no longer matches what you have, and every change after that is being judged against a page further out of date.

Snap to rounds every move to the nearest half, one or five millimetres, which is how you get a column of fields lined up exactly. Set it to Off for fine adjustments.

Everything can also be typed. Select an element and the panel on the right shows its exact position and size in millimetres, its font, size, colour and alignment, its border, and which page it appears on. Typing there is the way to make two elements agree precisely.

On a text element the height box is labelled Line height, because that is what it is: the height of one line, not of the whole element. See the note about dashed edges above.

Borders

Border width is in points, and 0 does not mean "no border" — it means a hairline, the thinnest line the printer can manage. Anything below about 0.25 comes out looking the same as 0 on paper. 1 is the usual thin rule, and is what is used when no width is set.

Draw it is what turns a border on and off, and it only appears for the elements that have one to turn off: text, values, address blocks, page numbers, the line-item table and barcodes. A box and a line are an outline — they always draw one — so their width and colour are the only settings that apply.

The border has its own colour, separate from the text. Leave it and the border follows the text colour; set it and the two are independent, which is how the shipped invoice draws black text inside a silver rule.

Adding, removing and overlapping

Add an element offers text, a value, a line or a box. New elements appear near the top-left corner — drag them where you want them. A value must be given something to show, chosen from the list of values this form can supply; nothing else can be typed in.

The arrows beside a selected element bring it forward or send it backward. That matters where two elements overlap: a filled box drawn after a piece of text hides it.

Delete removes the selected element.

Undo

Undo steps back through your changes one at a time, and Redo puts one back. Ctrl+Z and Ctrl+Y do the same. A drag counts as one change however far you moved the element, so undoing returns it to where it started rather than partway.

Inside a text box, Ctrl+Z is the box's own undo for the text you are typing, as usual.

Undo covers the changes you have made since opening the form, and that history is lost when you leave the page. It does not undo a save: to go back further than this, leave without saving and open the form again.

Saving

Save rewrites the form in the Forms format. If it was still in the older format you will see a note before you save: afterwards the original print engine can no longer open that form, and a copy of the original is kept alongside it.

The editor only saves what it can show you. Parts of a form it has no controls for — the individual lines of an address block, the columns of the line-item table — are kept exactly as they were.
